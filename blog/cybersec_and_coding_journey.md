Hi! My real name is **Fredmark Ivan Dizon**. I'm just a student. Nothing extraordinary — just someone who got curious, made mistakes, and kept learning. Yet sometimes people ask me, **_"How do you know all that at your age?"_**

I usually just smile, not because I feel proud, but because they only see the results. They see a project that works, a class discussion where I can speak up, a CTF scoreboard with my name on it, or a certification badge on my profile. What they don't see is everything that happened before those moments.

They don't see the quiet nights I spent staring at the same piece of code, trying to understand why it wouldn't work. They don't see the weekends I skipped just to experiment or finish something I wasn't required to do. They don't see the crashes, the corrupted files, the failed exam attempts, or the countless mistakes that made me question if I was even cut out for this.

I'm not going to sugarcoat this one. This isn't a highlight reel. This is the actual shape of the journey — the wins next to the burnout, the certs next to the nights I almost quit.

The truth is, there was no secret formula, no innate talent. Just curiosity, patience, and a decision to keep going, even when progress felt slow or invisible. I'm still learning, still figuring things out, and far from perfect. But over time, I've realized that **showing up, trying, and learning from failure** matters far more than any "shortcut" or natural gift.

This is my journey — not because I want to impress anyone, but because sharing what I've actually gone through, the ugly parts included, might help someone else understand that this path isn't about being perfect. It's about the small, unglamorous steps you take every day, even the ones nobody sees.

## My Coding Journey

I first started coding when I was in **Grade 10**. Honestly, it didn't start in a fancy lab or on a powerful computer — it started on my **phone**. I remember scrolling through tutorials, trying to understand simple logic, and feeling frustrated when even the smallest program wouldn't run. Looking back, those early experiments were clunky, slow, and full of errors — but they sparked something in me: curiosity.

I need to be honest about something here, because it's easy to leave out and it shouldn't be: learning to code on a phone wasn't a quirky origin story detail, it was a real limitation. I couldn't run most compilers properly. I couldn't test things the way tutorials assumed I could. I'd type out an entire program, hit "run," and get an error with no clear way to debug it beyond re-reading everything character by character. There were nights I genuinely felt behind before I'd even started — watching people online with real laptops, real IDEs, real internet speeds, while I was squinting at a phone screen trying to figure out why a semicolon mattered so much. That gap was real, and it never fully went away — it just became something I learned to work around instead of something that stopped me.

What inspired me most was my **uncle, who's an ethical hacker**. I would watch him work on his laptop, solving security challenges, writing scripts, and analyzing systems. I was amazed by how he could break down problems and make computers do what he wanted. At the time, I didn't understand the depth of cybersecurity or programming — but I knew I wanted to learn the way he did.

My first programming language was **C**. I started with the basics: printing text, simple loops, and learning how variables worked. I remember thinking that C was frustratingly strict. One missed semicolon or one wrong bracket could break everything. But that strictness taught me patience, attention to detail, and problem-solving — lessons I still carry today.

One of my first real projects was a **calculator program**. At first, it could only handle basic arithmetic: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. But as I learned more, I added extra features like a **currency converter, percentage calculator, and unit conversions**. That project was far from perfect, but finishing it gave me a sense of accomplishment I hadn't felt before. It taught me that even small ideas could grow into something more complex if I was willing to experiment and keep learning.

Learning on a phone had its challenges. Sometimes I couldn't test my code properly, or I had to rewrite entire programs because of formatting issues. I didn't have an instructor guiding me every step of the way. Most of what I learned came from **trial and error, reading online tutorials, and asking questions whenever I could**.

Over time, I realized that coding isn't just about writing programs — it's about thinking logically, solving problems, and learning from failure. Every small mistake taught me something: a syntax error taught me precision, a crashed program taught me resilience, and a confusing logic problem taught me persistence.

By the time I progressed to more advanced topics, I wasn't the fastest or the most knowledgeable, but I had something just as important: the habit of **learning, reflecting, and improving**. I started exploring other languages, experimenting with small projects, and even dabbling in cybersecurity challenges inspired by my uncle. I didn't aim to impress anyone; I just wanted to understand, explore, and grow, step by step.

## From Coding to Cybersecurity

At some point, curiosity about *how programs work* turned into curiosity about *how programs break* — and that's really the honest boundary between "I code" and "I do cybersecurity." It wasn't a clean transition. It was messy, and it started with getting absolutely destroyed by my first few CTFs (Capture The Flag competitions — cybersecurity challenges where you find hidden "flags" by exploiting deliberately vulnerable systems).

**Hack4Gov** was one of the first real competitive environments I threw myself into. It's a nationwide, government-run CTF here in the Philippines, organized by DICT's Cybersecurity Bureau and CERT-PH, with categories spanning web exploitation, digital forensics, OSINT, and more — the kind of event where regional qualifiers feed into national finals, and top performers get a shot at representing the country internationally. And here's the part I still have to remind myself actually happened: in my **first ever** Hack4Gov, our team took **Regional Champion**, and I placed **1st overall individually** in that regional leg. We carried that momentum to **Nationals**, where we placed **6th out of 20 teams** — the top teams from every region in the country, in the same room.

I want to be honest about what that result actually felt like in the moment, because "champion" and "top 1 individual" sound like a clean win from the outside, and it wasn't clean at all. Getting there meant months of getting wrecked on TryHackMe and HackTheBox boxes I didn't understand, entire practice sessions where I solved nothing, and walking into that regional round with zero proof it would work out — I didn't know I'd win until the scoreboard said so. And Nationals humbled me again immediately: 6th out of 20 is a real result I'm proud of, but it also meant standing next to five teams that were simply sharper than us that day, and having to sit with that instead of just celebrating the regional win.

I also competed in **Trend Micro** CTFs and **SEEN** CTFs. In the Trend Micro CTF, our team placed **2nd out of roughly 200 teams** in qualifiers, then made it to the finals as one of the **top 3 out of 5** teams that qualified nationally. In **SEEN**, we went all the way and took the **Championship**. Both of those wins came after stretches where we were nowhere near the top of any leaderboard — qualifying 2nd out of 200 didn't happen because we were naturally better than 198 other teams, it happened because we'd already absorbed the failure of a lot of earlier competitions before that one.

Somewhere along the way, TryHackMe and HackTheBox stopped being "extra practice" and became the actual backbone of how I learned. I've gone through **hundreds of labs and boxes** on both platforms — and I want to be clear about what that number actually represents, because it's not hundreds of clean wins. It's hundreds of attempts, most of which involved getting stuck, looking things up, failing again, and only occasionally landing that "oh, THAT'S how it works" moment that makes the previous four hours of frustration worth it. That grind is the part that doesn't show up on a scoreboard or a trophy shelf, but it's the actual reason any of those results happened at all.

Eventually that grind turned into certifications — and this is the part where I could easily make it sound smoother than it was, so I won't.

- **HTB CPTS (Certified Penetration Testing Specialist)** — an intermediate-level, fully hands-on certification where you have to actually compromise infrastructure and then write a commercial-grade penetration testing report, not just answer multiple-choice questions. I did not pass this on my first attempt. I want to say that plainly instead of quietly skipping over it, because I think the "I got certified" version of this story that leaves out the failed attempt is the version that makes other people feel like they're behind when they don't clear it the first time either.
- **HTB CWES (Certified Web Exploitation Specialist)** — focused specifically on web application penetration testing and bug bounty–style exploitation. This one meant relearning a lot of what I thought I already knew about web apps, because the exam expects you to chain vulnerabilities, not just spot one and stop.
- **HTB CAPE (Certified Active Directory Pentesting Expert)** — HTB's flagship Active Directory certification, built around enumerating and attacking enterprise Windows environments through multiple realistic attack chains. Active Directory was, and honestly still is, one of the topics that humbles me the fastest. There's a specific kind of frustration that comes from understanding a concept in theory and then completely losing the thread the moment it's live in front of you.
- **HTB COAE (Certified Offensive AI Expert)** — a newer certification covering adversarial attacks against AI/ML systems: adversarial machine learning, data poisoning, evasion attacks, and exploiting LLM outputs. Going into this one felt different from the others — it's a newer, less-charted part of the field, which made it exciting, but also meant there was a lot less "just Google it" available when I got stuck.

I'm not listing these to brag. I'm listing them because each one represents a specific stretch of my life that was genuinely hard, and because I think it matters to say: **certifications don't erase the doubt, they just happen alongside it.** I passed CWES and still had weeks afterward where I felt like an impostor going into the next CTF. That doesn't fully go away. You just get more practice functioning while it's there.

## The Part Nobody Posts About

I said I wouldn't sugarcoat this, so here it is.

There were stretches where I burned out completely — where I'd open my laptop, look at a lab or a box I needed to finish, and just... close it again. Not because I didn't care, but because I cared so much that the pressure of not being good enough yet became heavier than the curiosity that got me started. Burnout in this field doesn't always look like collapsing. Sometimes it looks like quietly disengaging for two weeks and telling yourself you're "taking a break" when really you're avoiding the feeling of failing again.

There were access and financial realities too, and I'm not going to pretend those don't matter, because they shaped a huge part of this journey. Not everyone starts with a proper laptop, a stable connection, or spare money for exam vouchers and subscriptions. I started on a phone because that's what I had. Later on, saving up for lab time, exam attempts, or even just staying subscribed to a platform long enough to finish a learning path was a real, recurring calculation — not a given. If you're reading this and your starting point looks like mine did, that's not a disqualifier. It's just a harder version of the same path, and it's still walkable.

And then there's failure, the plain kind. I've failed exam attempts. I've spent entire CTF windows scoring zero on a category I was sure I'd crack. I've rewritten the same broken exploit chain so many times I stopped counting. None of that made it into a highlight reel, but all of it happened, and honestly, most of what I actually know came from those specific failures — not from the moments things worked cleanly on the first try.

If someone tells you the path from "curious beginner" to "certified" is a steady upward line, they're either leaving things out or they got lucky. Mine wasn't. It looked more like a staircase built out of setbacks, with progress only visible if you zoom out far enough.

## Study Techniques That Helped Me

1. **Choose One Language and Stick With It at First**
   I started with C because I wanted to understand the basics. My advice is to **pick a language that fits your goals** and focus on it first. Trying to learn too many languages at once can be overwhelming and unreliable. Starting small allows you to **practice consistently** and master the fundamentals before moving on.
2. **Study Step by Step and Practice Consistently**
   Don't rush. Break your learning into small steps. I started with **variables, loops, and conditionals**, then moved on to functions, and finally small projects like my calculator. Each step gave me something concrete to **practice over and over**. Step-by-step progress builds **confidence and mastery**.
3. **Practice by Doing, Not Just Reading**
   At first, I spent a lot of time reading tutorials or watching videos, thinking that would teach me programming. But I realized real learning happened when I **typed out code myself, experimented, and broke things**. Every bug I fixed or feature I added taught me something that reading alone never could.
4. **Write, Test, and Rewrite**
   **Repetition was key.** I often had to **rewrite entire sections of code multiple times** to get them right. Each rewrite taught me better structure, cleaner logic, and patience. I learned that mistakes aren't failures — they're **opportunities to understand the problem more deeply**.
5. **Experiment with Your Own Projects**
   Tutorials are helpful, but personal projects make everything stick. I didn't just follow a calculator tutorial; I made it my own. I tried new features, changed the layout, and even added mini-tools like unit conversion. Experimenting like this helped me **connect concepts to real problems** and made programming more fun.
6. **Reflect on What You Learn**
   After finishing a project, solving a bug, or even failing an exam attempt, I'd take a moment to **think about what went well and what I could improve**. Reflection helped me see patterns in my mistakes and gradually avoid them.
7. **Use Resources, But Don't Rely on Them Completely**
   I learned a lot from online tutorials, forums, HTB Academy modules, and my uncle's guidance — but I realized that relying too much on them can lead to **"tutorial hell."** The key is to **use resources as support, not a crutch**, and always try to solve problems on your own first.
8. **Consistency Over Intensity**
   I didn't spend 12 hours a day coding, but I **spent time consistently**, even if it was just one or two focused hours. Small, daily practice helped me build momentum and improve steadily. Over weeks and months, those small efforts added up to real skills.
9. **Teach Others or Explain Out Loud**
   Whenever I could, I tried **explaining what I learned to friends or even to myself out loud**. Teaching others forces you to organize your thoughts and clarify concepts, and it often exposes gaps in understanding.
10. **Overcoming Laziness — and Overcoming Burnout, Which Is Different**
    I won't lie — there were many days I felt lazy, tired, or unmotivated, and starting anyway for just 10–15 minutes usually got me moving. But I want to separate that from burnout, because they need different responses. Laziness responds to "just start anyway." Burnout doesn't — pushing through burnout the same way I pushed through laziness just made it worse for me. What actually helped with burnout was stepping back honestly, admitting I needed a real break instead of a guilt-fueled forced session, and coming back when the curiosity felt like mine again instead of an obligation. Learning to tell the difference between the two took me longer than learning any programming language did.

## Misconceptions About Programming (and Cybersecurity)

1. **"You have to be a genius to code."**
   I used to think coding was only for people who were naturally smart. Watching my uncle solve security challenges made me feel like I could never catch up. But in my experience, coding is more about **curiosity and persistence than talent**. I've spent nights debugging the same code for hours, and I've seen other students who didn't think they were "smart enough" eventually build tools and projects that worked perfectly.
2. **"You need to be good at math to code."**
   Honestly, I've never liked numbers, and math was always frustrating for me. But most coding problems rely on **logical thinking, not advanced math**. Even when I added features like a currency converter or percentage calculations, it was mostly about **understanding formulas step by step**, not memorizing math.
3. **"Hackers/Programmers just type fast and know everything."**
   I assumed ethical hackers, like my uncle, were wizards who just "knew it all." The reality is that hacking and cybersecurity are mostly about **experimentation and patience**. My uncle often reminded me that **every hacker starts from zero**, and even he still spends time researching new vulnerabilities — it's about persistence, not speed.
4. **"Learning one language is enough."**
   When I started with C, I thought I could just stick to it and be done. But later, when I tried Python, Bash, and even some basic HTML/CSS, I realized that **each language teaches a new way to approach problems**.
5. **"Once you learn the basics, coding — or hacking — gets easy."**
   I thought that after learning loops, variables, and functions, programming would feel effortless. Then I passed a CTF or two and assumed the next one would feel the same. It didn't. Every new project, every new box, every new certification brought its own fresh wall to hit. It never actually gets "easy" — you just get better at getting through the hard parts.
6. **"You should only follow tutorials or copy code to learn."**
   I spent a long time in what I now call **"Tutorial Hell."** My first real progress came only when I **stopped copying and started experimenting on my own**.
7. **"Passing a cert or a CTF means you've made it."**
   This one's specific to the security side, and I learned it the hard way. Passing CPTS didn't mean the doubt disappeared. It meant I had proof I could do a specific thing, under specific conditions, on a specific day. The imposter feeling doesn't get a certificate. You just learn to keep working alongside it.

## A LITTLE ADVICE

If you want to learn programming or get into cybersecurity but feel lazy, intimidated, unsure where to start, or like your circumstances aren't "enough" — know this: **that's completely normal, but it also won't fix itself. You have to start anyway.** You don't need a perfect plan, a fancy computer, or to be a genius — I started on a phone and I'm not exaggerating that for effect. You just need to start small, focus on one language or one platform, and practice consistently.

Mistakes aren't failures — they're lessons. Failed exam attempts aren't the end of the story — they're a data point, not a verdict. Every crash, every bug, every rejected report, every CTF you score zero on teaches you something the wins never could.

Avoid trying to learn everything at once; depth and focus matter more than breadth. Use tutorials and guides, but don't rely on them — real learning happens when you **experiment, break things, and figure out solutions yourself**. And when burnout hits — and it will — don't confuse it with laziness. Rest without guilt, then come back.

Remember why you started, keep showing up, and push through the days that feel pointless, because consistent effort is what truly builds skills. Every small step compounds, and over time, those tiny actions add up to confidence, real progress, and the ability to achieve more than you ever imagined — certificates included, but never guaranteed, and never the whole story.

**Start now. Curiosity, persistence, and patience will take you farther than talent ever could — but they only work if you're honest with yourself about how hard it actually gets.**
