![The "Free 51GB" lure page](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*aCxuOcpLgdVr1CuA7avGPQ.png)

It starts with a message from a friend. A "Labor Day Special" promising **51GB of free data** to help you stay connected during the holiday. It's timed perfectly — people are traveling, celebrating, and their guard is down. But behind the friendly interface sits something much more deliberate: a **Phishing-as-a-Service (PaaS) kit** engineered to hijack your social circle and monetize your data and attention.

This writeup is a full teardown of how that kit works — not so you can build one, but so you can *recognize one instantly* the next time it lands in your messages. Understanding the machinery behind a scam is one of the most durable defenses against it, because the tricks repeat across campaigns even when the branding changes.

## Why This Matters

Scams like this one are common precisely because they work — and they work by exploiting *trust*, not technical weaknesses in your phone or browser. No app is hacked, no password is stolen through a software bug. The entire attack runs on convincing a human to click, share, and enter information voluntarily. That makes awareness — knowing the pattern, not just the domain name — the single most effective defense, because the domains and branding change constantly while the underlying psychological playbook stays the same.

## The Investigation

As a security researcher, the first move was to pull the link into a controlled lab environment rather than clicking it on a personal device. This matters: phishing kits are frequently designed to behave completely differently depending on who — or what — is visiting.

On a standard desktop browser, the site often appeared dead or simply redirected to a blank page. That's a **cloaking tactic** — a technique scam sites use to hide their real behavior from automated security scanners, antivirus crawlers, and researchers using ordinary desktop setups. Only once the investigation pivoted to a terminal and began **spoofing a mobile environment** did the page reveal its real structure. This alone is a useful thing to understand: the fact that a link "looks safe" when checked from a desktop or by an automated scanner is not proof that it's safe — it may simply mean the scam is choosy about its victims.

The first concrete step was running the URL through **urlscan.io**, a free service that renders a page in a sandboxed environment and records everything it loads — scripts, requests, redirects — without exposing a real device to risk.

![urlscan.io results for the campaign](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*D6sXUwXGNkz4e_sWvzvOzw.png)

One detail stood out immediately in the scan results:

![Script reference found in the scan](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1072/format:webp/1*SMO7lnqOjERiPdfE3w5A_Q.png)

A file called `script.js` was being loaded by the page. Retrieving and reading it (via `curl`, a command-line tool for fetching web content) turned out to be the key that unlocked the rest of the investigation.

![script.js retrieved via curl](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*azN0gInK4IxoN5UHfmvq8g.png)

## Layer 1: The Tracking and Anti-Analysis Script

At first glance, `script.js` looked like a modified copy of **Plausible Analytics**, a legitimate, privacy-focused web analytics tool many real websites use. That's not an accident — disguising malicious code as a well-known, trusted library is a common obfuscation technique, because a security-conscious victim (or a lazy automated scanner) who glances at the filename or a few lines of code may assume it's benign and move on.

In reality, this script served two purposes: **watching the victim**, and **hiding from researchers**.

### How it detects — and evades — researchers

```javascript
if ((window._phantom || window.__nightmare || window.navigator.webdriver || window.Cypress) && !window.__plausible)
    return s(null, e);
```

This is an **anti-analysis check**. Each of those properties (`_phantom`, `__nightmare`, `navigator.webdriver`, `Cypress`) is a fingerprint left behind by a specific browser-automation tool — PhantomJS, Nightmare.js, Selenium/WebDriver, and Cypress respectively. These are exactly the kinds of tools security researchers and automated scanners use to visit pages safely and record what happens.

If the script detects any of these fingerprints, it simply stops running — `return s(null, e)` exits before anything malicious triggers. The practical effect: a security scanner or a researcher using standard automation tooling may see a completely inert page and conclude the link is safe, while a real human visiting from a real phone sees the full attack unfold. This is precisely why the investigation had to spoof a real browser environment rather than rely on an automated scan alone — automated tools can be actively lied to.

### How it profiles the victim

```javascript
i.n = t,
i.u = a.href,
i.d = o.getAttribute("data-domain"),
i.r = r.referrer || null;
```

This builds an object (`i`) that bundles telemetry about the visitor:

- `i.u` — the full URL the victim is currently on
- `i.d` — which phishing domain/campaign this visit belongs to
- `i.r` — the **referrer**: where the click came from (Facebook, WhatsApp, SMS, etc.)

Collecting the referrer is a deliberate business decision by the attacker, not an accident: it tells them which distribution channel — which app, which shared post — is converting best, so they can invest more effort in whichever channel is working.

### How it exfiltrates the data

```javascript
n.open("POST", l, !0),
n.setRequestHeader("Content-Type", "text/plain"),
n.send(JSON.stringify(i));
```

This sends the collected profile to the attacker's `/api/event` endpoint. Notice the `Content-Type` is set to `text/plain` rather than `application/json`. This is a deliberate, technically clever evasion trick: browsers require an extra security check called a **CORS preflight request** before allowing certain cross-origin API calls — but `text/plain` requests are exempt from that check. In other words, the attacker chose a slightly unusual content type specifically so the browser treats a data-exfiltration request as if it were a harmless, ordinary page interaction.

### How it tracks multi-step flows

```javascript
window.history.pushState
```

By hooking into `pushState` (the browser API that lets a page change its URL/state without a full reload — the basis of most modern single-page apps), the script can track a victim moving between the different "stages" of the scam — landing page → verification → share prompt — even though the browser never technically leaves the page. This lets the attacker build a complete step-by-step funnel of how far each victim gets before dropping off, which is useful data for refining the scam over time.

## Layer 2: The Landing Page — Where the Manipulation Happens

The tracking script was only half the story. The next step was pulling the actual **HTML of the landing page** to find the real phishing logic — the form, the progress bar, and the "share to unlock" mechanic that does the actual psychological work.

![Landing page HTML source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*sdCKWyCV6qAM3Giv8ApMYw.png)
![Landing page logic continued](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*ewXNJVYn9NT1J05-yys0zw.png)

What looks, at a glance, like simple front-end UI code turns out to be a tightly engineered engagement funnel. Every piece of it is designed to manipulate behavior, not to deliver anything real.

### Persisting fake progress across visits

```javascript
var swidth = localStorage.getItem(saved);
if (swidth !== null) {
    var width = swidth * 1;
    $("#intro").fadeOut(0);
    $(".comments").fadeOut(0);
    $("#share").fadeIn(0);
    $("#fill2").css("width", width + "%");
    $("#percentage2").text(width + "%");
} else {
    var width = 0;
}
```

`localStorage` is browser storage that persists even after the tab is closed. This code checks whether the victim has visited before and, if so, **restores their previous fake progress bar** instead of starting over. This is a subtle but powerful psychological lever: it exploits the [sunk cost effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost) — the tendency to keep investing in something because of effort already spent — by making a returning visitor feel they're "almost there," even though the progress bar has never represented anything real.

### Turning victims into distributors

```javascript
$("#messenger").click(function () {
    window.location.href = share;
```

with `share` built as:

```javascript
share = "fb-messenger://share/?link=" + encodeURIComponent(landingDomain);
```

Clicking the "share" button doesn't unlock any reward — it opens Facebook Messenger with the phishing link pre-loaded, ready to send. This is the campaign's **propagation engine**. Every victim who shares becomes a distribution node, and because the link arrives from a real friend or contact rather than a stranger, it inherits an enormous amount of unearned trust. This is exactly why messages "from a friend" about too-good-to-be-true offers deserve *more* scrutiny, not less — the person sharing it is very likely a victim themselves, not someone vouching for it.

### The rigged progress bar

```javascript
if (width == 0) {
    width += 50;
} else if (width == 50) {
    alert(error);
    width += 15;
} else if (width == 65) {
    width += 5;
}
...
else {
    $("#share").fadeOut(0);
    $("#claim").fadeIn(1000);
}
```

This progression is deliberately **non-linear** and psychologically tuned:

- The first interaction jumps the bar by a large amount (`+50`) to create an immediate feeling of reward and momentum.
- Later increments shrink dramatically, and are interspersed with fake error messages:

  ```javascript
  error = "Sorry! You may have already shared this with the same friend or group. Please try again.";
  ```

  This "error" has nothing to do with any real system state — it exists purely to force additional shares and interactions. Combining a large early reward with small, interrupted later gains is a well-known engagement-design pattern (similar to variable-ratio reward schedules used in slot machines and some mobile games): it keeps people engaged far longer than a straightforward, honest progress indicator would, because the brain keeps expecting the reward is "almost" there.

### The fake payoff

```javascript
$("#share").fadeOut(0);
$("#claim").fadeIn(1000);
window.open(abcde, "_blank");
```

Once the fake progress bar finally "completes," a "claim" button appears — but instead of delivering any reward, it opens the `abcde` variable in a new tab, which redirects to the actual monetization destination (examined in the next section). There was never a reward to claim. The entire progress bar exists only to maximize how many share-and-click actions can be extracted from each visitor before they either fall for the final redirect or give up.

### Keeping the illusion alive

```javascript
startRedirectTimer();
setInterval(function () { ... });
window.onhashchange = function() { ... };
```

These functions maintain countdown timers, periodic updates, and navigation tracking that make the page feel "alive" and responsive — reinforcing the illusion of a real, functioning reward system throughout the interaction.

## Layer 3: Tracing the Infrastructure — Where the Money Actually Goes

With the front-end logic understood, the investigation shifted to the **supporting infrastructure**: where collected data ends up (the "dropzone"), and where victims are ultimately sent (the "final redirect" — where the actual monetization happens).

### The cloaked gateway

The `abcde` variable from the "claim" button pointed to a script called `go.php`.

![go.php response](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*drv-Lh-kXrVVFM3649me8g.png)

The response came back as a plain `200 OK` with **no `Location` header** — meaning, technically, no standard server-side redirect occurred. To an automated scanner checking only HTTP headers, this looks like a normal, static page load with no suspicious redirection behavior at all.

But the redirect still had to happen somewhere — and since it wasn't in the headers, it had to be in the **body** of the response, executed client-side. This is a well-known cloaking technique: hide the redirect inside JavaScript (`window.location.replace(...)`) or an HTML meta-refresh tag rather than an HTTP-level redirect, specifically so that simple scanners checking only headers see nothing suspicious, while a real browser executing the page's JavaScript gets silently forwarded onward.

**The general lesson here:** the absence of an obvious redirect in a quick technical check is not proof that a page doesn't redirect. Modern cloaking specifically targets the blind spots of casual or automated inspection.

### Finding the money page

Digging into the response body revealed the actual destination:

![Final redirect destination](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*J3EbZ2P0rXSWxXrhfx3X7Q.png)

The URL `https://free.gf920.com/` turned out to be the true monetization endpoint — not a reward page at all, but a **CPA (Cost Per Action)** landing page. CPA networks pay affiliates a small commission every time a referred user completes some action — filling a form, installing an app, viewing an ad, signing up for something. The URL carried tracking parameters (`utm_medium`, `utm_campaign`) that let the attacker measure exactly how well each stage of the campaign was converting.

The redirect itself used **two layers of redundancy** — a JavaScript `window.location.href` navigation *and* an HTML `<meta http-equiv="refresh">` tag as a fallback — ensuring victims still get redirected even if JavaScript is somehow blocked or fails.

Two other parameters were notable:

- A long tracking string (e.g., `b00cccbf...`) functioning as an **affiliate ID**, letting the operator claim credit — and get paid — for every converted victim.
- A campaign tag (e.g., `tang4g`) indicating the thematic lure used — in this case, mobile data/4G-related bait.

### The infrastructure behind it

A final check on the domain registration for the final payload site revealed useful attribution context:

![WHOIS/registrar information](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*T6r5-wGJIiLu3IPZKAsCuw.png)

The registrar was **Cloud Yuqu LLC (IANA 3824)**, a registrar commonly associated with cheap, high-volume domain registration and loosely-regulated ("bulletproof") hosting — patterns frequently seen in grey-market advertising and phishing operations, since such registrars tend to ask fewer questions and respond more slowly to abuse reports. The presence of `+86` (China country code) contact numbers suggested backend infrastructure managed from China-based services, consistent with patterns commonly observed in Phishing-as-a-Service ecosystems targeting Southeast Asia. Like the entry-point domain, the final payload was also fronted by **Cloudflare**, a legitimate content-delivery and DDoS-protection service — used here not for its intended purpose, but to add resilience against takedown attempts and hide the true hosting origin.

## The Full Attack Chain

Putting it all together, the campaign maps out as a single continuous pipeline:

1. **Lure** — A `.top` domain (`labor-day-51gb-free7.ij7gv.top`) presents the "51GB free" offer as a disposable, low-cost entry point.
2. **Telemetry & evasion** — `tj.16gift.com` tracks visitor behavior and filters out bots, automation tools, and researchers.
3. **Engagement funnel** — The obfuscated front-end logic manipulates victims through a fake, psychologically-tuned progress bar.
4. **Propagation** — Victims are coerced into sharing the link via Messenger, turning each one into a distribution node.
5. **Gatekeeping** — `go.php` acts as a cloaked validation/redirect step, invisible to header-only scanners.
6. **Monetization** — Victims land on `gf920.com`, a CPA advertising endpoint that pays the attacker per completed action.

| Role | Indicator |
|---|---|
| Initial Lure | `labor-day-51gb-free7.ij7gv.top` |
| Telemetry | `https://tj.16gift.com/api/event` |

Reporting the identified abuse contacts to the domain registrar, hosting provider, and Cloudflare's abuse reporting channels is a standard next step for researchers who want to support takedown efforts — though, as noted above, the choice of registrar in this case suggests the operators anticipated and are somewhat resilient to that process.

## Why This Campaign Exists: The Real Business Model

It's worth being explicit about *why* this works as a business, because that's what makes it dangerous and durable rather than a one-off scam. The intent isn't a single payday — it's a **multi-stage exploitation pipeline** built around three layers of monetization that reinforce each other.

**Layer 1 — Identity harvesting.** Prompting victims to enter a phone number "to check eligibility" builds a database of verified, real, active phone numbers — and crucially, numbers already known to belong to people actively seeking "free rewards." That behavioral signal is valuable on its own: these numbers are prime targets for **follow-up smishing (SMS phishing) campaigns**, often impersonating trusted financial or e-wallet services.

**Layer 2 — Affiliate/CPA fraud.** The final redirect isn't a reward — it's a paid conversion event. Each completed share-and-click cycle earns the attacker a small commission. The payout per victim is tiny, but the system scales through **forced virality**: every victim is pressured into recruiting more victims via their own trusted contacts, at zero advertising cost to the attacker.

**Layer 3 — Install-based or subscription deception.** The final "claim" step frequently pushes victims toward installing **potentially unwanted programs (PUPs)** disguised as cleaners or VPN tools that are actually adware, or toward silently activating **Value-Added Services (VAS)** — recurring paid subscriptions that quietly deduct small amounts from a mobile balance.

| Target | Purpose | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Phone number | Identity harvesting | Resold or reused for future targeted phishing |
| Social contacts | Viral propagation | Expands reach without any advertising spend |
| Final interaction | Affiliate monetization | Generates small but highly scalable revenue |

None of this requires hacking a device in the traditional sense. The entire attack runs on **voluntary human action** — clicking, sharing, typing a phone number — which is exactly why it can't be patched away with a software update. It has to be recognized and refused by the person looking at it.

The timing is deliberate too: launching around holidays (Labor Day, in this case) exploits a moment when people genuinely *expect* promotions and giveaways, which lowers baseline skepticism right when the lure needs it lowered most.

## How to Protect Yourself

The good news is that this entire category of scam shares a recognizable shape, regardless of the specific offer or domain name. A few habits go a long way:

- **Be skeptical of "too good to be true" offers that require sharing, verifying, or handing over data before any reward appears.** Legitimate telecom promotions do not require you to spam your contacts to unlock a benefit.
- **Don't enter your phone number on unfamiliar sites**, especially ones not hosted on a company's official domain. Check the actual domain in your address bar, not just the branding on the page.
- **Treat forced-sharing prompts as a red flag, not a feature.** If a page asks you to share via Messenger/WhatsApp/SMS before continuing, that's the propagation mechanism talking, not a genuine requirement.
- **Watch for artificial urgency and fake social proof** — rigged progress bars, fake "share counters," and manufactured error messages that demand you "try again" are manipulation tactics, not real system feedback.
- **Never install apps or APKs from outside official app stores**, especially ones offered as a "reward" or "bonus" at the end of a promotional flow.
- **Remember that a link coming from a friend doesn't make it safe.** In campaigns like this one, the "friend" who shared it is very likely a victim too, not a trusted source vouching for the offer.
- **When in doubt, go directly to the official source.** If your telecom provider is really running a promo, you'll find it on their official app, website, or verified social media — not a `.top` domain you've never heard of.

## Closing Thoughts

What makes campaigns like this genuinely effective isn't clever malware or a sophisticated exploit — it's a well-engineered psychological funnel wrapped around ordinary web technology (`localStorage`, `pushState`, CORS quirks, cloaked redirects), all aimed at converting a moment of holiday excitement into harvested phone numbers, viral distribution, and small recurring ad payouts. Understanding the mechanics — the fake progress bar, the forced sharing, the cloaked redirect chain — doesn't just satisfy curiosity; it's what lets you spot the *next* one instantly, even when it's wearing completely different branding.

If a promotion requires you to share it, install something, or click through an artificially stalling progress bar before revealing a reward — it isn't a reward system. It's a monetization trap built around your attention, your identity, and your trust in the people you know.

Stay skeptical out there.
